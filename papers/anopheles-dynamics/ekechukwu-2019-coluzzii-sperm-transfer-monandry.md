# Current versus future reproductive investment adaptive responses in adult *Anopheles coluzzii* malaria mosquitoes: hydric-stressed males give it all

**Authors:** Ekechukwu NE, Tripet F
**Journal:** Parasites & Vectors | **Year:** 2019 (12:377) | **DOI:** 10.1186/s13071-019-3608-3
**File:** papers/anopheles-dynamics/ekechukwu-2019-coluzzii-sperm-transfer-monandry.md

---

## Abstract (summary)

Laboratory experiments (Keele University, 2011–2012) on the **Mopti strain of *An.
coluzzii*** quantifying male sperm transfer and female sperm uptake/maintenance under
hydric (water-deprivation) stress. The paper's introduction is the most compact
statement available of the anopheline mating system: males are sperm-limited, females
are largely **monandrous**, mating happens **in swarms at dusk**, and each male can
inseminate only a few females per night.

## Key Results (as used by the model)

- **Males can inseminate at most ~3 females per night**: "Male *An. coluzzii* can only
  inseminate ~3 females per night and are limited in the amount of sperm and the size
  of the mating plug they can transfer per female" (the cited source is the authors'
  own G3 2015 paper, ref. 24). **[M]**
- **Mating is a swarm phenomenon, once in a lifetime**: "females are also largely
  monandrous and visit swarms initiated by males at dusk once in their lifetime to
  mate" (ref. 23 = Tripet et al. 2003, AJTMH; **not open access — [NO-VERIFICADO]**).
- **Males are the limiting sex in the swarm**: "females are clearly the limiting sex
  and sexual selection on males is particularly intense with approximately **7 % of
  males successfully pairing on any given night**" (ref. 25 = Diabaté et al. 2011 —
  see `diabate-2011-gambiae-swarms-mating-success-mali.md`). **[P]**
- **Males swarm several consecutive nights but mating carries an energetic cost**:
  "Males can swarm for several consecutive nights"; "mating carries large energetic
  costs which translate in decreased future survival in males which swarm for extended
  periods of time"; females instead become refractory after a successful insemination.
- **Female mate choice is weak**: females "appear to accept any incoming males".
- **Measured insemination rate in cages (Mopti strain, laboratory):**
  - male-stress experiment, **50 males : 100 females** (1:2) overnight:
    **52.6 % of virgin females inseminated** (χ² = 0.04, df = 2, P = 0.978, n = 587);
  - female-stress experiment, same 1:2 ratio: **40.8 % inseminated**
    (χ² = 19.9, df = 2, P < 0.001 vs the male experiment; treatment differences
    not significant, P = 0.081, n = 581).
- **Sperm transferred per mating (qPCR, Y-chromosome copies in the spermatheca):**
  control **2 856 cells** (95 % CI 1 516–4 197), 4 h hydric stress **5 303**
  (3 009–7 597), 8 h stress **10 598** (7 308–14 000) — stressed males transferred
  **3.7×** the sperm of non-stressed males.
- **Sperm stored per female** varies roughly **1 700–7 000 cells**; **7 days after
  mating** the females retained **~4× fewer sperm cells** than at mating, with median
  sperm activity score 3–4 (medium to high).
- **Sperm must survive for months**: "In *An. coluzzii*, sperm viability needs to be
  maintained for much longer durations because females sometimes need to store sperm
  for several months of estivation during the dry season before reinitiating
  oogenesis when the rainy season begins" (ref. 47). **[M]**

## Relevance to MalariaSentinel (Centinela)

This is the **only *A. coluzzii*-specific quantification of the male side of the mating
system**, and it is the paper that decides the male-module question:

1. **The male's epidemiological role is fertilisation, and only fertilisation.** The
   male transfers sperm and a mating plug; the plug makes the female refractory and
   triggers ovogenesis. There is no evidence here that the male affects female biting
   rate or female survival directly — the only measurable male→female link is sperm.
2. **A female that is not inseminated produces nothing.** Female monandry + a swarm in
   which only ~7 % of males pair per night means the *per-female insemination
   probability* is the quantity that must be in the model if males stay an aggregate.
3. **The aggregate is defensible at the level of a single gonotrophic cycle**: with
   ~3 females inseminated per male per night, one male is "enough" for ~3 females in
   one evening. The male population therefore only needs to be tracked as a *ratio* and
   a *per-female insemination probability*, unless the spatial distribution of males
   (swarm markers, swarm density) is itself the thing being modelled.
4. **What a per-female insemination probability cannot express**: the *seasonal* and
   *spatial* coupling between swarm availability (dusk, markers, 1–3 m above ground)
   and female mating opportunity, and the fact that females mate **once, at dusk,
   before their first blood meal** (cross-referenced in
   `charlwood-2003-funestus-swarming-mating-mozambique.md` and
   `bimbile-somda-2018-arabiensis-swarming-mating-burkina-faso.md`: 98.47 % of
   females caught in copula were inseminated and only 2.97 % were blood fed).

**Caveats:** laboratory, colonised Mopti strain, cage mating (1:2), Keele insectary;
sperm transfer is not the same as realised fertilisation; the two headline numbers
(~3 females/male/night; ~7 % of males pairing per night) are **quoted from other
papers** (refs 24 and 25) rather than measured here.
