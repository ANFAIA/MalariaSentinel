# Spatial distribution and male mating success of *Anopheles gambiae* swarms

**Authors:** Diabaté A, Yaro AS, Dao A, Diallo M, Huestis DL, Lehmann T
**Journal:** BMC Evolutionary Biology | **Year:** 2011 (11:184) | **DOI:** 10.1186/1471-2148-11-184
**File:** papers/anopheles-dynamics/diabate-2011-gambiae-swarms-mating-success-mali.md

---

## Abstract (summary)

190 swarming sites of *Anopheles gambiae* (M molecular form) mapped and quantified in
the village of Sokourani, Niono district, Mali (Sahelian rice-irrigation area),
August–September 2008, by 30 observers surveying at sunset for 10 days. The paper
quantifies **where and when swarms form**, **how big they are**, and **how mating
success is distributed among males** — the core measurements needed to build a swarm
representation of the male population.

## Key Results (as used by the model)

- **Timing (relative to sunset):** "Swarms were typically observed **1–3 m above
  ground** at the same site every evening"; swarming **started ~5 minutes after
  sunset** with one or two males in zigzag flight; numbers rose sharply within 5 min
  and **peaked 9–15 min** after the first male; the swarm was nearly empty
  **25–30 min** after the first male appeared. **Couples were usually observed from
  6 min after the first males started to swarm and continued for the next 10 min.**
- **Species composition of swarms:** **97.4 %** of the 190 swarms were mono-specific
  *An. gambiae*; mixed swarms (~95 % *An. gambiae*) contained *A. rufipes*,
  *A. pharoensis* or *Culex* spp.
- **Swarm size:** peak swarm size was multimodal — main modes near **100** and **180**
  males, secondary modes near **360** and **420**, possibly one near **690**.
- **Marker type does NOT explain swarm size** (F = 0.40, P = 0.26) and neither does
  date (P = 0.155); **swarming site explains 47.6 %** of the variance in swarm size
  (P = 0.003) — i.e. the *place* matters, the *landmark type* does not.
- **Mating success:** **1 456 mating couples observed in 70 swarms** at 30 sites over
  11 days; couples per swarm ranged **0–93** (mode 6). Swarm size predicted mating
  success linearly and strongly (F = 57.9, P = 0.001); swarm duration did not.
- **Per-male mating probability per night: 0.072 (SE 0.0095), i.e. ~7 % of the males
  in a swarm mate each evening**, and the slope means **one additional mating event
  for every 14 additional males** in the swarm. "individual male success is similar
  regardless if he joins a large or small swarm."
- **Spatial pattern:** swarms were concentrated along the village residential area
  (higher swarm density where human habitations are), consistent with the
  **"hotspot" model** of lek formation; the median distance between a swarm and its
  nearest neighbour was **10 m** (25 % of swarms < 6.5 m); swarms did **not** cluster
  around high-mating-success swarms.
- Mean male wing length **2.81 mm**; no significant variation in male body size
  between swarms.

## Relevance to MalariaSentinel (Centinela)

The single most useful source for **swarm geometry and swarm-level mating
statistics**, but note it is *An. gambiae* s.s. (M form) in Mali, not *A. coluzzii*
in Ghana → evidence tier **[P]**.

- It gives the **swarm as a marker-anchored, dusk-timed, short-lived entity**:
  a swarm occupies a point, lasts ~30 min after sunset, is 1–3 m above ground, and
  its size is set by the site (47.6 % of variance) rather than by the marker.
- It gives the **decisive number for the male aggregate**: **7.2 % of males per swarm
  per night** obtain a mating. If males stay an aggregate in the Centinela, the
  per-female insemination probability is the derived quantity (≈ 0.072 × number of
  males available to the female, capped at 1).
- It shows that **swarm size and total mating are linearly coupled**, so a
  "swarm density" field (males per unit area per night) is a sufficient statistic for
  the aggregate option — it captures the spatial structure without individual males.
- **Caveats:** net straight-line distances, one Sahelian village, M-form
  *A. gambiae*; swarm size was estimated by photographing and counting at 3-min
  intervals; the ~7 % figure assumes each male mates at most once per evening.
