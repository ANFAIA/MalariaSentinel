# DNA profiling of human blood in anophelines from lowland and highland sites in western Kenya

**Authors:** Thomas W. Scott, Andrew K. Githeko, Andrew Fleisher, Larissa C. Harrington, Guiyun Yan
**Journal:** The American Journal of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene | **Year:** 2006 | **DOI:** 10.4269/ajtmh.2006.75.231
**File:** papers/anopheles-dynamics/scott-2006-dna-profiling-multiple-blood-meals-western-kenya.md

---

## Abstract (summary)

PCR-based DNA profiling of human blood meals in *Anopheles funestus* and *An. gambiae*
collected from human habitations at lowland and highland sites in western Kenya. The study
quantifies how blood meals are distributed among household members and, crucially, how
often a single mosquito takes blood from more than one person within one gonotrophic
cycle.

## Key Results (as used by the model)

- "**Multiple blood meals in a single gonotrophic cycle occurred frequently enough to be
  epidemiologically important (14 % for *An. funestus* and 11 % for *An. gambiae*).**"
  → species-resolved field rates for the *gambiae* complex and for *funestus*, both of
  which the engine models.
- "Less than 20 % of human hosts contributed to > 50 % of all blood meals, and 42 % were
  not bitten at all, including people in the age group bitten most often." → the
  distribution of bites over hosts is highly skewed; a multi-meal female therefore
  disproportionately contributes to the *same* individuals within a household.
- "As expected, bites were unevenly distributed by age (young adults > older adults >
  children)." "Use of untreated bed nets by adults, but not children, seemed to redirect
  bites to children."
- "Mosquitoes that did not bite a person who slept in the collection house can affect
  estimation of entomological risk." → the denominator problem: the number of bites per
  gonotrophic cycle must be estimated from blood-meal analyses, not from biting counts.

## Relevance to MalariaSentinel (Centinela)

Gives two of the three species-relevant field numbers for the multi-meal design:
11 % (*An. gambiae*) and 14 % (*An. funestus*) of females take more than one human meal
per gonotrophic cycle. Together with Norris et al. 2010 (18.9 %, *An. arabiensis*, Zambia)
and Das et al. 2017 (23–26 %, Zambia) this brackets the field rate for the engine at
roughly 0.1–0.25 of females per cycle.

Caveats: western Kenya (not Ghana), 2006, and the estimates are for *gambiae* s.l. and
*funestus* rather than *coluzzii*; the abstract does not report meal volumes.
