# Genetic evidence for the importance of interrupted feeding by mosquitoes in the transmission of malaria

**Authors:** David J. Conway, J. S. McBride
**Journal:** Transactions of the Royal Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene | **Year:** 1991 | **DOI:** 10.1016/0035-9203(91)90217-m
**File:** papers/anopheles-dynamics/conway-mcbride-1991-genetic-evidence-interrupted-feeding-malaria.md

---

## Abstract (summary)

Genetic (allelic serotype) argument for interrupted feeding by mosquitoes. *P. falciparum*
isolates were obtained from 17 pairs of Gambian children living in the same house and
presenting with malaria at the same time; the frequency of allelic serotypes of MSP1, MSP2
and Exp-1 in the local area was used to compute the probability that two randomly chosen
isolates would be genetically identical.

## Key Results (as used by the model)

- "the probability of a random pair of isolates containing an identical genotype was
  calculated to be **less than 0.01**. However, **3 of 8 household pairs in one year, and
  6 of 9 in the next year, contained identical *P. falciparum* genotypes**, a much higher
  frequency than expected randomly (P < 0.00005, for each year)."
- "This finding is discussed in terms of the **probable contribution of single mosquitoes
  infecting more than one person**."

## Relevance to MalariaSentinel (Centinela)

The strongest *field* (as opposed to laboratory) argument that a single mosquito
infects more than one person within a household — i.e. that "interrupted feeding" /
multi-meal behaviour has a measurable epidemiological footprint. It justifies modelling
multiple human contacts per female per night (and per gonotrophic cycle) and, crucially,
the *consequences* of that: the mosquito carries parasites from one host into the blood
meal taken from the next.

Caveats: 1991, The Gambia (not Ghana); the paper is an inference from genotype
coincidence, not a direct observation of feeding behaviour, and the number of pairs is
small (8 and 9). It gives no volume or threshold information.
