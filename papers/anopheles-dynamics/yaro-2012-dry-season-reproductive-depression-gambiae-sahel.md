# Dry season reproductive depression of *Anopheles gambiae* in the Sahel

**Authors:** Alpha Seydou Yaro, Adama Traoré, Diana L. Huestis, Abdoulaye Adamou, Seydou Timbiné, Yaya Kassogué, Moussa Diallo, Adama Dao, Sékou F. Traoré, Tovi Lehmann
**Journal:** Journal of Insect Physiology | **Year:** 2012 | **Volume/Pages:** 58(8): 1050–1059 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jinsphys.2012.04.002 | **PMID:** 22609421
**Open access URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4789105/ (NIHMS author manuscript, NIHMS763299)
**File:** papers/anopheles-dynamics/yaro-2012-dry-season-reproductive-depression-gambiae-sahel.md

## Abstract (summary)

Wild-caught Sahelian M-form *An. gambiae* from a **xeric (Sahelian)** and a
**riparian** population in Mali are compared across the wet and dry seasons for
oviposition response, egg batch size, egg development, insemination rate and
blood-feeding response. This is the key quantitative source for **dry-season
reproductive depression**: females take blood meals as usual, but they do not
lay — they either arrest ovarian development (gonotrophic dissociation) or retain
fully developed eggs without laying them.

## Key Results (as used by the model)

- Dry-season persistence mechanism and its length: "Adults of the M form of
  *An. gambiae* persist through the long dry season, when no surface waters are
  available, by **increasing their maximal survival from 4 weeks to 7 months**.
  Dry season diapause (aestivation) presumably underlies this extended survival."
- "**Oviposition response** in the Sahelian M form **dropped from 70% during the
  wet season to 20% during the dry season** while the **mean egg batch size
  among those that laid eggs fell from 173 to 101**. Correspondingly, the
  fraction of females that exhibited **gonotrophic dissociation increased over
  the dry season from 5% to 45%**, while a similar fraction of the population
  **retained developed eggs despite having access to water**."
- "This depression in reproduction … was **not caused by a reduced insemination
  rate**." "**Blood feeding responses did not change with the season** in either
  population."
- Seasonally resolved oviposition response (Sahelian population; oviposition
  response = "the proportion of gravid females that **laid eggs within 3 d after
  water was provided**"): "it fell from **60% in October-December to 20% in
  January-May**, before it increased to 50% in June, and then to **70% in
  July-August**" (P < 0.001, df = 4, n = 207, ML χ² test).
- Gonotrophic dissociation by period (Sahelian M form): "the fraction of females
  exhibiting gonotrophic dissociation during the dry season (**January-February:
  53%** and **April-May: 37%**) is higher than that of the wet season (the
  remaining of the year; 4-33%)"; "during the dry season in the Sahel,
  **35-50% of the females that did not lay eggs exhibited gonotrophic
  dissociation, whereas the remaining 50-65%, developed eggs but they did not
  lay them**."
- Insemination rate "was relatively high throughout the year (**65%**) in the
  Sahelian M form"; "the insemination rate was **higher in the dry season (82%
  in April-May)** than in the mid rainy season (67% July-August)" (not
  significant, P = 0.28).
- Feeding response by gonotrophic state: "The magnitude of the differences
  between species amounted to **two- and three-fold between the unfed and
  gravid females**"; "The interaction between gonotrophic state and species was
  not significant (P > 0.8), nor was the main effect of gonotrophic state
  (P > 0.07)."
- Relation to the pre-gravid state: "The relatively low oviposition response of
  the riparian population in the early wet season (July-August) was possibly due
  to high recruitment of **newly-emerged pre-gravid females, many of which
  require two blood meals prior to the maturation of the first egg batch**
  (Charlwood et al., 2003; Hogg et al., 1996; Yaro et al., 2006)."
- Seasonality caveat for the oviposition assay itself: "In the early wet season,
  this index [gonotrophic dissociation] was probably **overestimated** because of
  the high frequency of newly-emerged females".

## Relevance to MalariaSentinel (Centinela)

- **Provenance for the dry-season reproduction parameters**: oviposition
  probability (0.20 dry vs 0.70 wet), egg batch size (101 vs 173 eggs) and
  gonotrophic-arrest fraction (5 % → 45 %) for the Sahelian *An. gambiae* s.s.
  (M form) — the closest measured proxy for *coluzzii* in the same region.
- **A key negative result for the model**: "**Blood feeding responses did not
  change with the season**". → the dry-season slowdown of the *vector* channel is
  a **reproductive** effect (no eggs ⇒ no oviposition ⇒ no new larvae), **not** a
  feeding-rate effect. So the engine should not reduce the biting rate in the dry
  season; it should reduce the conversion of blood → eggs → oviposition.
- **Provenance for a two-way dry-season branch in the female state machine**:
  (i) *arrested* females (gonotrophic dissociation: fed, no oocyte maturation) and
  (ii) *retaining* females (fully developed eggs but not laid — the female is
  "holding" a batch and will oviposit only when water appears). Both are
  non-lethal and both postpone reproduction; they are different states and the
  Centinela's `PRE_GRAVID`/arrest design should distinguish them.
- **Provenance for the "gonotrophic dissociation" concept used by Mamai 2026**
  and for the pre-gravid (two-meal) first cycle.
- Caveats: *An. gambiae* s.s. M form (Sahel, Mali), not *coluzzii* (note that
  Mamai et al. 2026 re-read the same result as "gonotrophic dissociation in
  *An. coluzzii* collected indoors during the dry season in an arid region of
  Mali"); the oviposition
  assay is a 3-day "does she lay when water is provided" proxy, not a natural
  oviposition delay measured in days from the blood meal; the xeric vs riparian
  contrast shows the depression is **population-specific** (riparian: no dry/wet
  difference in oviposition response, P = 0.68).
