# Effect of sugar on male *Anopheles gambiae* mating performance, as modified by temperature, space, and body size

**Authors:** Gary RE, Cannon JW, Foster WA
**Journal:** Parasites & Vectors | **Year:** 2009 (2:19) | **DOI:** 10.1186/1756-3305-2-19
**File:** papers/anopheles-dynamics/gary-2009-male-sugar-survival-swarming-mating.md

---

## Abstract (summary)

Laboratory experiments quantifying the effect of **plant-sugar feeding on male
*Anopheles gambiae*** survival and mating performance, as a function of temperature
(23 °C and 27 °C), body size (large vs small, from rearing density) and space
(cage size). Males have no blood-meal source, so sugar is their only fuel; the paper
asks whether sugar is optional or essential, and measures the survival and swarming
consequences.

## Key Results (as used by the model)

- **Male laboratory lifespan is short and strongly sugar-dependent** (mean ± SE):
  without sucrose at 23 °C large males **3.72 d** vs small males **2.99 d** (P < 0.0001);
  at 27 °C large **2.36 d** and small **1.94 d** (P < 0.0001). A **single 20 % sucrose
  meal** extended life by slightly more than 1 day (23 °C: **4.94 d**; 27 °C: **3.84 d**,
  P < 0.0001 for large males; small males 3.77 d and 3.15 d).
  "In all survival experiments, the **majority of males were dead by 3.5 days**";
  the longest survival without sugar was **4.5 days** (a few large males at 23 °C).
- **Swarming requires sugar at 27 °C but not at 23 °C:** swarming was significantly
  more common with sugar at 27 °C (χ² = 73.01 and 87.13 for large and small males,
  P < 0.05) and at 23 °C in small males (χ² = 9.13, P < 0.05), but **not** for large
  males at 23 °C (χ² = 0.54, P > 0.05).
- **Swarming starts on the second night after emergence** (not night 1), and by night 3
  the proportion swarming was higher with sugar (χ² = 20.01 and 71.54).
- **Antennal fibrillae** (the male's hearing organ) are erected just before scotophase
  and for ~**1 hour** (from the start of swarming to just after it) on night 2 with
  sugar; without sugar at 27 °C only 20 % of remaining males erected fibrillae and
  for **< 40 minutes**. Starved males had **no erect fibrillae by night 3** and none
  lived through the night.
- **Body size:** large males (2.87 ± 0.02 mm wing length, reared at 100/pan) vs small
  males (2.61 ± 0.02 mm, 1000/pan) — significant (P < 0.0001); larger males live
  longer and mate better.

## Relevance to MalariaSentinel (Centinela)

Evidence tier **[P]** (*A. gambiae* s.l., laboratory, forced-rearing; **not**
*coluzzii*; the strain is not stated).

This is the closest published quantification of the **cost of swarming on male
survival** — the mechanism behind the qualitative statement in
`ekechukwu-2019-coluzzii-sperm-transfer-monandry.md` that "mating carries large
energetic costs which translate in decreased future survival in males which swarm for
extended periods of time". It contributes three things the draft can use:

1. **A mechanistic statement of the male survival channel**: males live on sugar alone,
   and the swarm is a *sugar-dependent* activity (the same males that swarm are the
   ones that survive). Therefore a male's daily survival is **not** a species constant
   equal to the female's: it is a function of **sugar availability and of swarming
   effort**, and the Centinela's male survival should be a **separate parameter from
   the female's**, not the same 0.87/0.93.
2. **An age structure for the male layer**: males begin swarming on **night 2 after
   emergence** and (with sugar) continue; the swarm activity window is
   **~1 hour after the start of scotophase**, which is an independent confirmation of
   the ~27-minute swarm duration measured in the field (the hour includes the whole
   swarming bout, not only the peak).
3. **A falsifiable expectation**: if the Centinela ever models male sugar feeding
   (e.g. as part of a sugar-bait intervention), male survival should respond to it,
   and swarm participation should fall when sugar is unavailable — the only published
   *testable* coupling between the male layer and an intervention.

**Caveats:** laboratory, forced-rearing, no wild individuals; the numbers are
lifetimes in a cage (days), not field daily survival, and are **not** comparable with
the field MRR daily survival of 0.773 ± 0.094 (Epopa 2017); the males here are
*G. gambiae* s.l., not *coluzzii*; the experiment does not measure mating success
directly beyond insemination rates in cages; sugar was a single 20 % meal, not a
continuous supply.
