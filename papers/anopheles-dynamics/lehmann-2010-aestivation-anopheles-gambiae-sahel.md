# Aestivation of the African malaria mosquito, *Anopheles gambiae*, in the Sahel

**Authors:** Tovi Lehmann, Adama Dao, Alpha Seydou Yaro, Abdoulaye Adamou, Yaya Kassogue, Moussa Diallo, Sékou Traoré, Cecilia Coscaron-Arias
**Journal:** The American Journal of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene | **Year:** 2010 | **Volume/Pages:** 83(3): 601–606 | **DOI:** 10.4269/ajtmh.2010.09-0779 | **PMID:** 20810827
**Open access URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2929058/
**File:** papers/anopheles-dynamics/lehmann-2010-aestivation-anopheles-gambiae-sahel.md

## Abstract (summary)

Demographic study plus mark–release–recapture spanning the end of one wet season
to the beginning of the next in Sahelian villages of Mali, resolving the
60-year-old question of whether the Sahelian malaria vector survives the dry
season by **aestivation** (in situ, dormant) or by **long-distance migration**
from sites where breeding continues. Conclusion: aestivation.

## Key Results (as used by the model)

- Operational definition used by the authors: "a mosquito that **survives over 3
  months** during the dry season when no larval sites are available **can be
  defined as aestivating**."
- The decisive observation: "Initially captured, marked, and released at the end
  of the previous wet season, she has **survived the 7-month-long dry season**";
  "Photographs of the **aestivating female recaptured after the first rains
  nearly 7 months (212 days) after being marked**."
- Population signal: "**Five days after the first rain, before a new generation
  of adults could be produced, mosquito abundance surged 10-fold**, implying
  that most mosquitoes were concealed locally until the rain." "Four days after
  the first rain, a marked female *An. gambiae* s.s. was recaptured."
- Context of the dry season: "In dry savannas, where surface waters required
  for larval development are **absent for 4–8 months per year**. Under such
  conditions, *An. gambiae* virtually disappears."
- Why the mechanism matters for control: "Because aestivating mosquitoes are
  probably found in every village where larval sites are unavailable for over 3
  months, **interruption of aestivation must cover large clusters of villages**
  to prevent migration from untreated villages nearby."
- Historical caveat the authors note: "the key findings in support of
  aestivation, namely extended longevity (over 3 months) and **gonotrophic
  dissociation**, have not been reproduced despite repeated attempts" — i.e.
  gonotrophic dissociation (the arrested-ovary state) is part of the *definition*
  of the aestivation phenotype used here.
- Baseline mortality for contrast: "adults seldom survive beyond 2 months"
  (outside aestivation).

## Relevance to MalariaSentinel (Centinela)

- **Establishes the survival budget a dry-season female can spend**: aestivating
  *An. gambiae* survive up to **212 days (7 months)** in the Sahel, i.e. the
  engine's daily adult survival (0.87–0.93) applied for 212 days is physically
  impossible for a normal mosquito; the dry-season survival of the adult
  population must be modelled as a **separate, much lower daily mortality with
  a state-specific (aestivating) branch**, not as the ordinary
  temperature-driven curve.
- Supports the semantic choice that **a dry larval habitat is not lethal for the
  adult female**: the strategy is *postponement* (gonotrophic dissociation +
  concealment), with the population re-emerging within days of the first rain.
- Provides the operational definition to encode in the model:
  "aestivation = female surviving > 3 months with no larval sites, with arrested
  ovarian development".
- Caveats: *An. gambiae* s.s. (M form) in Mali; the primary evidence is one
  recaptured marked female plus a 10-fold abundance surge; the paper does not
  report blood-feeding rates or oviposition delays during aestivation (those
  come from Yaro et al. 2012 and Mamai et al. 2026).
